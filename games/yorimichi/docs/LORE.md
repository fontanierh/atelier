# Lore

The working draft of Yorimichi's story and setting, in plain language, to be edited freely. Nothing here is final.
The name Yorimichi (寄り道) means a detour on the way home; the city Hidamari (日溜まり) is a sunlit spot.

The direction: a normally peaceful world that spirits are invading for the first time. Multiplayer at MMO scale with
action-brawler combat, many quests and dungeons. One continent-sized island first, a second continent later. People
live everywhere. Spirits come from chasms and other openings in remote places, and keep out of cities for a real
reason. The way to get rid of them is discovered over time: every city has a bell, and all must ring at once.

Avoid: a child-versus-adult framing, "someone forgot the festival" as the cause, a single bell keeper and a single
villain as the spine (too much of a solo game), and cute spirits.

## The island

One large island. A spine of high mountains runs down the middle. West of it, the coast in the game today: forest,
fishing villages, Hidamari. East and inland: high plains, a great lake, river towns, terraced farming valleys, a
mining region, a cold northern plateau, a hot southern cape. The coast is fishermen and traders. The mountains are
miners, monks and guides. The plains are farmers and horse people. Inland cities are older and prouder than the coast
and do not think of the coast as the centre of anything. A second continent across the sea is a later expansion.

## The openings

Places where the ground has split and does not close: a chasm high in the central range, a sinkhole deep in the
western forest, a whirlpool off the south cape, a crater lake on the northern plateau, a cave system under the mining
region. Every opening is remote. Nobody lives near one by choice. The nearest town to each is the poorest and
strangest in its region, and it is where trouble starts. The weather over the central range is theirs now; the sky
is not an opening, it is a sign.

## Why now

**The island sits on top of them.** Long before anyone's records, the island belonged to the spirits. The first
settlers came by sea, fought them for generations, and did not win. They sealed. They drove the spirits into the cold
places under and behind the island, closed every opening with a lock of cast metal, and hung bells of the same metal
in every settlement. Then they forgot. The locks became strange old shrines in places nobody goes. The bells became
town bells that ring the hours. The story survived only as scraps: a nursery rhyme, the oldest book of the plateau
monastery, and the inscription cast into every bell, which everyone takes for decoration.

**What the bells are for.** The seal has two parts: a door and a key.

- *The locks are the doors.* One at each opening, cast from the metal, set into the rock. While a lock is whole,
  nothing comes through. The openings are connected underneath, so a lock does not hold on its own; all of them hold
  together. Break one and the pressure moves onto the rest.
- *The bells are the key.* The settlers could not stay at the openings forever, so they built a way to close all the
  doors at once from where people lived. Every settlement got a bell of the same metal. When every bell on the island
  rings in the same hour, the locks set, all together, because they only hold together. Each town was responsible
  for its own bell.
- *Between closings, the bells are the fence.* The metal repels spirits and the sound drives them back. The settlers
  hung the bell in the middle of town and rang it often so nothing that leaked through would come near the houses.
  The habit outlived the reason: towns kept ringing the hours.

Discovery in the game runs in that order: bells keep them out; bells are a set; the set closes the doors, but only if
every bell rings, and some bells are gone.

**Mining broke it.** The metal of the locks and bells is rare and valuable, and the richest vein runs under the
central range. The mining guild has dug it for forty years, and melted old bells when a town would sell. This summer
a deep gallery broke into the cave that holds the mountain lock. The miners cut through it for the ore. That night
the cold came up through the mine, and within a month the other openings had cracked too: one broken lock puts the
pressure on all the others. The guild knows what it did and has told no one.

**Why they are angry.** Two thousand years in the dark and the cold on the wrong side of a door, and the island was
theirs. They are not visiting. They are taking it back, and they treat people the way we would treat something that
moved into our house while we were away. Whether the settlers were right to seal them is a question the game asks
later, not at the start.

## What the spirits are

Not one people. The small ones are the animals of the other side: crow things, fox-masked hunters, leaf-wraiths,
rope-and-driftwood things that were once drowned men. They hunt, steal and nest. The tall ones are something else:
one for each opening, old enough to remember the sealing. Indifferent to people the way weather is. Each holds the
ground around its opening and pushes outward.

They carry their cold with them. Near an opening the fog does not lift, leaves fall and never rot, frost creeps down
in a season that should not have frost. Two mountain towns are empty. A fishing fleet from the south cape did not
come back. People who went into the forest sinkhole came out weeks later, well fed, calm and wrong. They say the
spirits made them an offer.

## Why they stay out of cities

**What people think.** Nobody knows. It has been a few months, the news is bad, and the island is full of theories.
Most are wrong, a few are funny, and one is right and nobody listens to her.

- They cannot cross a paved road. Cities have paved roads. Case closed.
- It is the smell of fish. Coastal towns are fine, therefore fish. Inland cities are buying salted fish by the cart.
- They are afraid of cats. Hidamari has a lot of cats.
- The city councils have paid them off, which is why they only attack villages. Popular in the villages.
- They only take people who deserve it. Popular with people who have not been taken.
- It is the mine. Something came up out of the mine. Nobody can prove it, and the guild pays for a lot of
  newspapers.
- An old bell-ringer in a plateau town says they never come while the hours are being struck, and she has not missed
  an hour in fifty years. Everyone agrees she is a sweet old thing.

Rumours give quests a voice: a farmer wants forty cats, a city council hires you to disprove the mine theory, and the
one true rumour sits in the pile from day one for anyone who checks it.

**What is true.** It is the bells. The metal repels them, and a bell that rings drives them back as far as it can be
heard. Every old town rings its bell every hour to tell the time and has been unknowingly keeping itself safe since
the breach. Places founded after the story was lost have no bell: the mining towns, the frontier villages, the
hamlets of the western forest. Those are falling. The first town lost to the frost sold its bell to the guild for
scrap three years ago.

**The gradient.** Spirits carry their cold with them and weaken the farther they get from an opening. Near one they
are strong and the tall ones can walk; far from one they are thin, fast raiders that strike and pull back. The wilds
are theirs, the cities are not, and the map gets worse as you walk toward a chasm.

In the game's world: Hidamari's clock hall rings the hours and is safe without knowing why. Momiji Hamlet has no bell
and will be lost unless players hold it. The island temple bell has been a curiosity for a century.

## Discovering the mechanism

The spine of the live game. Not one quest chain: what the whole player base learns in stages, each of which changes
what the world does.

1. **Survive.** Fight what comes out of the woods, hold the villages, chase the rumours.
2. **The bells.** A village survives a night it should not have because someone rang for help. A town that sold its
   bell is found empty. Bell defence becomes the first big group activity.
3. **The set.** The inscription is the same on every bell on the island and the plateau monastery can read it. The
   bells are one instrument. There are locks at the openings. One was cut. The guild's secret comes out, and so does
   the older truth about who was here first.
4. **The hour.** Every bell, one night, one hour, and the openings close. But bells are missing, melted years ago,
   and the only metal to recast them is in the guild's hands. First server-wide event: recast the bells, hold every
   tower, ring as one.
5. **After.** The openings close and the spirits fall back. Not gone: locked again, and now everyone knows locks can
   be cut. Then the question the settlers never answered: what is on the other side, and were we right. That is the
   second continent.

## The human side

- **The guild** caused it, knows it, and is the only source of the metal that hurts spirits. It sells bell-metal
  blades, pole-heads and lantern frames to anyone who can pay. Rich, armed, and not giving up the mines to make
  bells. Every city needs it and half of them hate it.
- **The wardens** are whoever steps up: the monastery, the coastal towns, guides and fishermen, the old bell-ringers
  who kept ringing out of habit. They have the knowledge and no metal.
- **The ones who came back** from the openings are the seed of a third side, if wanted. What the spirits offer and
  what it costs is a mystery line for later.

## Rules to keep the tone

- Spirits are dangerous and strange, never cute. No blood or gore; beaten spirits break apart into leaves, fog, rope
  and driftwood and drift back toward the openings.
- Residents are never enemies by default; the human conflict is political and can become faction play.
- Every tall one has a reason. Every dungeon is a place people stopped going.

## Open questions

- Is the guild a playable faction, or a powerful faction everyone deals with?
- Is "the island was theirs first" known from the start, or revealed at stage three (the current lean)?
- Names: the island, the other side, the openings, the tall ones, the metal, the bell night, the guild, the wardens
  are placeholders. Keep names short and half-Japanese, half-plain, like Hidamari and Momiji Hamlet.
